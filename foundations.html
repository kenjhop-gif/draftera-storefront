The framework

Draftera Foundations

A framework for using AI well in nonprofits and social enterprises

01

Purpose

Most nonprofits now use AI, but few have decided how. Imagine Canada’s 2026 research found that 80% of Canadian nonprofits use AI, yet only 10% have a formal AI policy, and 64% of those using AI have no policy and are not developing one.

Nonprofits hold sensitive information about donors and the people they serve, and they depend on public trust. Draftera Foundations sets out, in plain language, what an organization needs in place to use AI safely, transparently and to real benefit. It is designed for lean teams and gives them a clear place to start.

02

Who it is for

Draftera Foundations is written for the boards, executive directors and staff of nonprofits and social enterprises, particularly small and mid-sized organizations without dedicated legal or IT staff. It was developed in the Canadian context and draws on Canadian standards and law, but its pieces apply to mission-driven organizations elsewhere.

03

The six pieces

Four pieces concern governing AI well, and two concern using it well. An inventory and annual review hold them together.

  • Leadership and policy

    The board has approved an AI policy, a named person is responsible for it, and staff have plain rules on which tools to use and for what.

  • People and skills

    Board members understand AI well enough to govern it, and staff can use it safely and recognize its errors.

  • Data and privacy

    Clear rules set out what information stays out of AI tools, and each tool is checked for how and where it stores data.

  • Human oversight

    A named person reviews AI output before it is used, with closer review where decisions affect people, and results are checked for fairness.

  • Transparency and trust

    Donors, funders and the public are told plainly where and how AI is used.

  • Value and measurement

    AI is applied to real tasks, and the results are measured so that decisions rest on evidence.

  • Inventory and annual review

    The organization keeps a current list of where AI is used and reviews every piece once a year as tools and laws change.

04

Evidence base

Each piece is included because it appears both in recognized AI governance frameworks and in what nonprofits, donors and funders report they need.

  • International frameworks

    The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and the OECD AI Principles.

  • Canadian standards and guidance

    CAN/DGSI 101, the National Standard of Canada for the ethical design and use of AI by small and medium organizations; DGSI 135, a digital capability assessment framework for nonprofits; and the joint principles on generative AI issued by Canada’s privacy regulators. A detailed mapping of each piece to CAN/DGSI 101 and DGSI 135 is in progress.

  • Sector evidence

    Imagine Canada’s 2026 research on AI adoption in Canadian nonprofits, donor research on transparency, and sector frameworks from Fundraising.AI and NTEN.

05

How organizations use it

  1. Start with one piece.Most organizations begin where the need is greatest, often a policy, board training or one practical workflow.
  2. Build the whole.Over time, the pieces add up to a complete approach to AI that is reviewed once a year.
  3. Seek independent verification, if desired.An organization with the pieces in place is positioned to have its practices independently verified against CAN/DGSI 101 through the Digital Governance Council. Draftera helps organizations prepare. It does not certify them and is independent of the Council.

06

Principles

  • People stay responsible

    AI supports staff and does not replace their judgment.

  • Plain language

    Every piece is written for lean teams without a legal department or IT staff.

  • Documented evidence

    Each piece leaves a record, from board minutes to training logs to measured results.

  • Independence

    Draftera Foundations is not tied to any technology vendor.

07

Scope and limits

Draftera Foundations is practical guidance. It is not legal advice, and following it does not guarantee compliance with any law or standard. Organizations should seek legal and cybersecurity advice where their circumstances call for it.
